Adaptive Arity and TreeCover Migration in Hybrid
TreeKEM
Mohammadreza Parsania and Dr Hamid Mala
Faculty of Computer Engineering, University of Isfahan, Isfahan, Iran
Abstract
Hybrid key establishment increases the communication cost of tree-based group messaging, while changing the
tree arity introduces a separate cost of rebuilding shared state. This paper investigates a migration policy for
hybrid TreeKEM that increases arity from two to four and, conceptually, from four to eight. Each valid node
carries classical and post-quantum key material. During migration, the new tree becomes the sole source of the
group commit secret, while an authenticated cover of eligible old-tree nodes temporarily delivers new path
secrets. Subsequent commits populate the new tree, and normalized delivery costs guide retirement of the old
cover. An analytical model accounts for public-key and encapsulation payload separately from other protocol
fields. Under its healthy-tree approximation, increasing arity from two to four at 129 members reduces the
modeled payload from 12,672 to 6,976 bytes, a 44.9% reduction. A leaf-only initial transition at 1,024
members instead requires eleven additional healthy commits to recover its modeled excess cost. Security is
examined through conditional arguments rather than a formal proof. Retained old keys can prolong group-wide
exposure after compromise, so a communication-cost threshold does not establish cryptographic recovery. The
contribution is a migration design and explicit accounting of its efficiency and security tradeoffs.

1 Introduction
Messaging Layer Security (MLS) establishes shared cryptographic state for dynamic groups through
authenticated membership changes and tree-based key updates [1]. A commit can refresh a path toward
the root and distribute the necessary path secrets to other members. When usable internal keys exist,
several members can receive a secret through one subtree key. Blank or invalidated nodes require a finer
recipient resolution and can increase communication. Hybrid classical and post-quantum key
establishment makes the cost of these public keys and encapsulations particularly relevant.
Multi-recipient key encapsulation mechanisms (mKEMs) reduce repeated ciphertext material when the
same secret is sent to several recipients. Their application to TreeKEM, including trees of degree greater
than two, is established prior work [2]. Tree shape and degree have also been studied as optimization
parameters for MLS [3]. These results motivate a different operational question: how can a group change
its tree arity without immediately rebuilding every shared internal key, and how should it decide when the
retained old structure is no longer useful?
We investigate the adaptive design developed in the first author's master's thesis [4]. A transition
introduces a new tree whose internal state is initially sparse. Eligible nodes of the old tree form a
temporary TreeCover for delivering path secrets belonging to the new tree. Only the new root supplies the
commit secret. As commits populate the new tree, delivery becomes less dependent on the old cover. A
retirement policy compares the new and old structures against leaf-only and idealized delivery costs.
The contribution is the combination of an explicit migration lifecycle, temporary old-tree delivery, and a
cost-based retirement rule. It is not a new KEM, the first use of hybrid cryptography in MLS, or the first
proposal for higher-arity TreeKEM. We present the thesis construction as the authoritative design and
derive its communication tradeoffs under explicit assumptions. The numerical examples are direct
evaluations of the analytical formulas. The scope is protocol design, cost analysis, and conditional security
reasoning.
Three distinctions organize the analysis. First, a healthy-tree byte formula accounts for selected
cryptographic components rather than every protocol field. Second, lower costs for a mature tree do not

2 Background and related work
2.1 Tree state and hybrid delivery
An MLS ratchet tree associates members with leaves and distributes private state according to subtree
membership. Path derivation and recipient encryption have different roles: path secrets determine future
node keys and the commit secret, whereas encryption delivers selected path secrets to members. This
separation is retained in the proposed design. The hybrid output is an encryption input for path-secret
transport; it is not itself the group epoch secret. The term hybrid in Hybrid Public Key Encryption denotes
a KEM–KDF–AEAD composition [5]; it does not by itself mean a classical/post-quantum combiner.
Integration must ultimately use the authenticated group context and the full MLS key schedule [1].
The IETF work on ML-KEM and hybrid MLS cipher suites provides a separate route to post-quantum
deployment. Revision 06, dated 21 July 2026, specifies cipher-suite choices and remains an Internet-Draft
[6]. The Amortized PQ MLS Combiner instead couples two MLS sessions and imports exported post-
quantum material into the traditional session [7]. Revision 02 is an expired draft, dated 20 October 2025.
Neither draft is an RFC. Our migration retains two structural versions temporarily, but does not run
distinct classical and post-quantum MLS sessions.
2.2 Multi recipient encryption and tree degree
Alwen et al. explicitly analyze mKyber for TreeKEM and for an m-ary variant [2, Section 4]. Sharing a
large common ciphertext component makes additional recipients comparatively inexpensive.
Consequently, a reduction in path depth may compensate for the greater number of siblings at each level.
Their treatment already establishes the architectural motivation for higher arity. Our remaining question
concerns migration between arities and management of the old keys during that process.
Chevalier et al. study tree evolution and degree selection for MLS [3]. Their reported preference for
ternary trees under some post-quantum parameter sets reinforces that the useful degree depends on the
cost model. The thesis restricts its candidate degrees to two, four, and eight; this restriction does not
establish global optimality. Table 1 positions the present work relative to the closest approaches. A
broader comparison should use the same topology assumptions and cost model across several candidate
degrees.

2.3 Established KEM security and protocol scope
KEM combiners have established security guarantees under precise component assumptions [8]. The QSF
framework underlying X-Wing gives complementary confidentiality reductions: one relies on KEM IND-
CCA security; the other on KEM ciphertext second-preimage resistance (C2PRI) together with the stated
Diffie–Hellman assumption [9, Section 6]. It provides a framework for analyzing other KEM components
when those hypotheses hold. Similarly, the symbolic TreeKEM proof establishes guarantees for its
specified MLS protocol [10]. FIPS 203 specifies ML-KEM [11]; mKyber has its own construction and
security analysis [2].
mKyber enjoys standard non-adaptive multi-user IND-CCA security [2, Sections 2.3 and 3.1]. For
ciphertext binding, Alwen et al. prove that their explicit-rejection Fujisaki–Okamoto (FO) mKEM is
collision resistant whenever its output hash is collision resistant [12, Theorem 5]. In the corresponding
accepted-key profile, deterministic re-encryption fixes the recipient ciphertext for each recovered
message. A different accepted ciphertext matching an honest target key must therefore yield a different
message with the same output hash. This gives recipient-local C2PRI even with the recipient secret key
exposed, under the corresponding classical or quantum hash assumption. The property concerns the
complete canonical recipient ciphertext; it does not assert that a recipient detects changes to other
recipients’ ciphertext components.
Adaptive-corruption confidentiality is a separate property. The distinction between mKyber and amKyber
concerns that stronger multi-user model and the available reduction bounds [2]; it does not negate
mKyber’s standard IND-CCA guarantee or the FO ciphertext-resistance result. For this article, the
remaining task is to match the selected rejection and output rules and the hybrid derivation in Equation (3)
to the applicable theorem, then analyze recipient eligibility and state evolution during migration. Primitive
security results provide the basis for this protocol analysis; they do not alone establish group recovery with
retained historical keys.
3 Model and cryptographic specification
3.1 Participants and state
Let n be the number of active members, e the epoch, and d the current arity. The candidate arities are 2, 4,
and 8. A public node has a location and, when valid, a classical public key and a post-quantum public key.
A member retains the private keys needed along its authorized path. A blank node has no usable shared
node key. A node may also become ineligible because its secret was available to a removed member, even
if a public key remains stored.
During migration the state comprises an old tree, a new tree, a fixed mapping between member identities
and new leaves, a tree version, and authenticated transition metadata. The old tree is a delivery structure
only. The new root is the unique authority for deriving the commit secret. At most one arity transition is
active. This logical separation is needed to prevent receivers from treating a retained old root as an
alternative source of epoch state.
The adversary may observe, delay, reorder, replay, or modify delivery traffic and may obtain member
state through compromise. Confidentiality cannot be expected against an authorized member that currently
knows the group secret. Statements about recovery concern a former compromise after the adversary has
ceased obtaining fresh state, with authentication credentials and recipient selection satisfying the stated
assumptions. No availability guarantee is asserted against a delivery service that suppresses messages.

On accepting a transition commit, members agree on the target arity, tree version, leaf mapping, and
transition state. The new internal nodes begin blank; the initiating commit materializes its direct path. For
every recipient subset needing a particular new-tree path secret, delivery first uses eligible new-tree nodes,
then eligible old-tree cover nodes, and finally individual current leaves. The choice operates over subsets,
not as a global minimum between two scalar cost totals.
Algorithm 1. Starting and advancing a migration
1. Check the authenticated current state, policy conditions, and absence of another active transition.
2. Fix the target arity and identity-to-leaf mapping; bind them to the next accepted group state.
3. Initialize the new tree and derive fresh secrets on the committer's new direct path.
4. Partition each intended recipient set using eligible new nodes, eligible old cover nodes, then current leaves.
5. Encapsulate per nonempty level and encrypt each required path secret under the thesis hybrid construction.
6. Authenticate and process the commit; reject inconsistent versions, mappings, recipients, or derivations.
7. Continue deriving group state from the new root and reevaluate cover costs after accepted changes.
Algorithm 1 summarizes the thesis lifecycle at the design level. The precise cryptographic encodings
remain to be specified. Figure 1 illustrates the distinct functions of the two tree versions: an old subtree

A removal invalidates affected path keys in both retained structures before selecting recipients for the next

Welcome mechanism; they cannot be assumed to know old internal keys. This join behavior is a design

Removing leaves can make an apparently mature tree less useful for the next committer. Updating one
branch also does not populate every other branch. Migration progress should be assessed using the
recipient sets actually under consideration, with an explicit aggregation convention if a group-wide
statistic is desired. Node counts alone do not determine delivery cost.

5 Conditional security analysis
5.1 Confidentiality and authentication obligations
The intended hybrid property is that path-secret confidentiality survives failure of one key-establishment
component. Published KEM and combiner theorems provide the relevant primitive guarantees, including
the FO ciphertext-resistance result discussed in Section 2.3 [8, 9, 12]. Applying them to (3) requires a
precise adversarial game and a correspondence between its KDF, encapsulation mechanisms, encodings,
and validity checks and those of the selected theorem. Correct authenticated encryption, authenticated
public keys, and independent component randomness are additional obligations. A complete
computational proof for the composed migration protocol remains to be supplied.
The transition adds an authentication obligation beyond encapsulation. Members must agree on the active
arity, root authority, tree version, frozen mapping, and applicable old keys. Otherwise, even an honestly
encrypted path secret could be interpreted in different trees. A receiver should accept state changes only
after validating the commit and its bound transition context. A precise proof would have to connect these
6

PAGE 7
checks to a group-state agreement invariant across adds, removals, updates, and retirement. The existing
symbolic proof for standard TreeKEM cannot simply be reused [10].
The hybrid encapsulation design does not itself specify a post-quantum replacement for classical
signatures. A post-quantum confidentiality aspiration therefore must not be described as full protection
against an active quantum adversary capable of forging the retained classical signatures. In particular, the
design must distinguish a passive adversary recording traffic for later decryption from an attacker that can
already impersonate members. Credential compromise and credential recovery also require their own
model.
5.2 Forward secrecy and retained state
Forward secrecy requires protection of earlier traffic after later state compromise. One-way path
derivation and erasure of obsolete state support this goal only under an appropriate key-evolution
argument. TreeCover deliberately extends the lifetime of some old private keys. A later compromise of a
retained key may expose recorded migration ciphertexts addressed to that key. Whether those recovered
path secrets expose a particular earlier epoch additionally depends on the key-schedule state available to
the adversary. Retention therefore changes the relevant exposure window and cannot be dismissed as a
harmless storage optimization.
A complete protocol needs explicit key lifetimes and a record of which key generations were available to
which members. It must invalidate removed-member knowledge, erase superseded state when no longer
needed, and reject reuse of a retired version. Removing retained state does not establish that no earlier
copies exist. The available evidence does not provide a general secure-erasure theorem.
5.3 Post compromise recovery can be group wide
Consider an attacker that copied a member's state, including an old cover private key and the relevant
preceding epoch state. If a later commit still encrypts a useful new-tree path secret to that old key, the
attacker can recover it and derive upward to the new root. With the necessary previous key-schedule
inputs, the attacker can continue deriving group epoch state. The disclosure is not confined to the leaves
below that old node: the recovered root contribution affects the group's common secret.
Consequently, recovery requires that the attacker lose access to every delivery route capable of revealing
the fresh contribution, under the relevant compromise scenario. An honest update is insufficient if the
same contribution remains accessible through an attacker-known cover key. Continued compromise or an
authorized malicious recipient prevents the desired recovery regardless of arity. This is a conditional
argument about secret dependencies.
Neither H nor H measures adversarial knowledge. A high H can coexist with one unsafe retained
new old new
route, and a low H says little about the confidentiality of the routes still used. A security-driven

update scheduling, honest entropy injection, and elimination of compromised routes, no finite bound on

6.3 Occupancy and asymptotic limits
The thesis also considers a simplified occupancy argument. For a fixed subtree of s members in a static
group of n, after t independent uniformly chosen member updates, the probability of at least one update in
that subtree and its associated one-node-versus-leaves cost are
p (t) = 1 − (1 − s/n)t, E[c (t)] = 1 + (s − 1)(1 − s/n)t (14)
s s
This model assumes that an update in the subtree makes its relevant aggregate key usable and that no later
removal invalidates it. Summing the second expression requires an explicit set of disjoint relevant
subtrees. It does not justify using the same distribution after conditioning on “effective” updates, nor does
it predict costs for arbitrary churn. The formula therefore supplies intuition for gradual population only
under the stated sampling and key-validity assumptions.
For fixed arity, storing the public tree takes O(n) space and a member's path private state takes O(log n)
space. Retaining old and new structures preserves these asymptotic orders while increasing constants.
Complete-tree information in a Welcome can remain linear in n. Similarly, a commit's upload size and a
delivery service's total broadcast volume are different: transmitting a full B-byte object to n receivers can
cost approximately nB bytes.
7 Discussion and limitations
The design shifts part of an arity change from an immediate rebuild into subsequent ordinary commits.
TreeCover provides a concrete way to make that shift: old internal keys can reduce leaf-level delivery
while the new tree acquires usable internal state. The same retained keys prolong dependencies on
historical secrets. Communication efficiency and compromise recovery must therefore be treated as
separate objectives, even when both motivate refreshing tree state.
The analytical savings show where the chosen parameter dimensions can make a higher arity attractive.
They do not determine the best arity or threshold. A broader analytical treatment should include ternary
and other degrees, exact occupied topology, heterogeneous update frequencies, repeated removals, and
cumulative costs over both growing and shrinking groups. Fixed-degree, immediate-rebuild, and
TreeCover policies should be compared for the same mathematical sequence of group changes, with setup,
Welcome, and retirement costs included.

The most urgent protocol work is to finalize the thesis combiner, its encodings, and the state-transition
rules. A security model must track historical node-key knowledge during migration, distinguish passive
post-quantum confidentiality from active authentication, and define the conditions for recovery. A formal
treatment should include authenticated tree-version agreement, removal eligibility, and atomic retirement
at each receiver. The present conditional analysis identifies these obligations without claiming that they
have been discharged.
The analytical scope also has limits. The healthy reference replaces exact occupied topology by a rounded-
height approximation, while the break-even examples assume immediate healthy operation after transition.
A tighter analysis would bound the cumulative delivery cost while the new tree remains sparse and would
specify how changes in membership affect those bounds. Such a result is not supplied by the present
examples.
8 Conclusion
Adaptive arity in hybrid TreeKEM is useful only if the costs and security consequences of changing the
tree are included. The thesis design makes the new root authoritative immediately and uses an old-tree
TreeCover to deliver new path secrets during gradual population. Its reference model exhibits lower
mature-tree key-and-KEM payload at selected larger arities, while the transition examples show that
repaying initial cost can require many later commits. These analytical examples do not prove the
construction secure or establish that one migration policy has lower cumulative cost for every sequence of
group changes. TreeCover is therefore best understood as a migration mechanism whose efficiency policy
must be complemented by explicit key-freshness, authentication, and recovery conditions.
